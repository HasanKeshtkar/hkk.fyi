Problem · 01

Millions live with a brain that misfires.

When the drugs stop working, the target sits deep inside the brain.

In Parkinson's, essential tremor and epilepsy, a small group of neurons deep in the brain fires in the wrong rhythm. Medicine helps many patients — but not all of them, and often not forever. Then the faulty circuit itself has to be reached.

Solution · 01

Deep Brain Stimulation works.

Electrodes at the target. The tremor stops.

A surgeon places a thin wire right inside the faulty circuit. A pacemaker-like device in the chest sends it small, fast pulses that override the bad rhythm.

Solution · 01

A few volts, right where it matters.

Parkinson's · tremor · dystonia · epilepsy — in clinics since the 90s.

The contacts sit inside the target, so only a few millimetres of tissue around them are stimulated. That precision is why it works so well.

Problem · 02

But it needs brain surgery.

skull openinginfection · bleedinghardware for life

A hole is drilled in the skull, the wire passes through healthy brain, and the hardware stays for life. So the next question is: can we reach the same deep spot without opening the head?

Solution · 02

Temporal Interference.

Stimulate deep — from the scalp.

Two pairs of electrodes are simply placed on the head — no surgery. The trick is in the two frequencies they use.

Solution · 02

Two kHz fields. Neurons ignore each one.

2000 Hz from one pair, 2010 Hz from the other.

A field that flips thousands of times per second is too fast for a neuron to follow, so each field on its own does almost nothing.

Solution · 02

Where they meet, a 10 Hz beat is born.

Neurons follow the beat — only where both fields overlap.

Two tones 10 Hz apart add up to one tone that swells and fades 10 times a second — like two guitar strings slightly out of tune. Neurons do follow that slow beat, and it is strongest where both fields are strong.

Two frequencies, one beat.

The weaker field sets the envelope. Nothing else.

E₁ and E₂ are how strong each pair's field is at one point in the head. The beat there is twice the weaker one — so it is big only where both fields are strong.

Problem · 03

The hotspot is wide.

≈ 30 mm across a 70 mm phantom. The neighbours get stimulated too.

Both fields are fairly strong over a large region around the centre, so the beat is strong there too. The usual fix is more electrodes and more channels. We wanted to sharpen it by changing only the waveform.

Solution · 03 · our work · ICEE 2026

PM-TI: one carrier, opposite phase modulation.

PM-TI · 1
s1(t)=E1cos⁡(ωct+φ(t)),s2(t)=E2cos⁡(ωct−φ(t))
φ(t)=arccos⁡(cos⁡(ωmt)k),k≥1,ωm≪ωc

Both electrode pairs now share one frequency. Their phases are pushed in opposite directions by φ(t), and the number k sets how hard. With k = 1 this is exactly classic TI. With k > 1 the beat stays the same where the two fields are equal — at the target — and shrinks wherever they are not.

envelope · E₁ ≠ E₂
E₂ / E₁0.60

The beat at a point where E₂ is weaker than E₁. At E₂/E₁ = 1 all three are equal; the lower it goes, the more k > 1 cuts the beat.

Unequal fields get penalised. k turns the penalty up.
No new electrodes — a new waveform.

Solution · 03 · one number to tune

Turn k up. The focus tightens.

Same electrodes. Same current. Same peak.

k is just a number in the signal generator. Raising it from 1 to 3 shrinks the hotspot width (FWHM: where the beat is above half its peak) from about 30 mm to 9 mm, while the beat at the target stays as strong.
